# Advertising Hooks

## Evidence and Claims Rule

Examples in this file are illustrative unless explicitly identified as real case studies.

When generating hooks:
- Never invent statistics, revenue figures, customer counts, conversion rates, testimonials, or performance results.
- Only use specific numerical claims when they are provided by the user or supported by verified evidence.
- If an example contains a number purely to demonstrate a formula, treat the number as fictional and do not reuse it as a factual claim.
- Do not create false urgency, false scarcity, or misleading guarantees.

## Purpose of a Hook

The hook is the first part of an ad designed to earn attention and create enough interest for the viewer to continue watching, reading, or listening.

A strong hook should usually do at least one of the following:

- Create curiosity
- Present a desirable outcome
- Call out a painful problem
- Make the viewer feel personally identified
- Introduce something unexpected
- Challenge an existing belief
- Promise useful information
- Show proof or credibility
- Create an emotional reaction

The hook should be relevant to the product and should connect naturally to the rest of the ad.

A hook that gets attention but has little connection to the product may increase views while hurting conversions.

# Core Hook Principles

## 1. Lead With the Customer, Not the Product

Weak:
"Introducing our new posture corrector."

Better:
"If your back starts hurting after sitting for 3 hours, watch this."

The viewer usually cares more about their own problem or desired outcome than the product itself.

Focus on:

- Their frustration
- Their goal
- Their fear
- Their curiosity
- Their current situation

Then introduce the product as the solution.

---

## 2. Specificity Makes Hooks Stronger

Specific hooks often feel more believable and relevant than vague ones.

Weak:
"This will improve your skin."

Better:
"If your makeup starts separating around your nose by noon, this may be why."

Weak:
"Save time every day."

Better:
"This cut about 20 minutes out of my morning routine."

Specific details can include:

- Time
- Situation
- Audience
- Symptom
- Behavior
- Result
- Number
- Context

Avoid fake precision or fabricated statistics.

---

## 3. Create Curiosity Without Becoming Clickbait

A curiosity hook gives the audience enough information to become interested, but leaves an unanswered question.

Examples:

- "I was making this mistake every morning without realizing it."
- "There is one reason your ads may be getting clicks but no purchases."
- "I changed one part of my landing page and immediately noticed a difference."
- "Most people use this completely wrong."

The information withheld should eventually be revealed.

Do not create curiosity around something the ad never delivers.

---

## 4. Use an Open Loop

An open loop introduces an unfinished idea that the viewer wants resolved.

Example:

"Three things were destroying my ad performance. The third one surprised me."

The viewer now wants to discover all three.

Open loops work especially well in:

- Short-form video
- Tutorials
- Educational ads
- Storytelling
- Product demonstrations

Do not delay the payoff so long that the viewer loses interest.

# Major Hook Types

## 5. Problem Hook

Immediately identify a painful or annoying problem.

Formula:

"If you struggle with [specific problem], this is for you."

Examples:

- "If your lower back hurts after sitting at your desk all day, watch this."
- "If your foundation always looks cakey by lunchtime, try this."
- "Still spending hours manually following up with leads?"

Best when:

- The problem is common
- The audience recognizes the problem immediately
- The product has a clear solution

---

## 6. Desired Outcome Hook

Lead with the result the customer wants.

Formula:

"How to get [desired outcome] without [undesirable sacrifice]."

Examples:

- "How to make your videos look more expensive without buying another camera."
- "How I started eating more protein without cooking three meals a day."
- "A faster way to follow up with every lead without hiring another employee."

This works well when the desired outcome is strong and easy to visualize.

---

## 7. Curiosity Gap Hook

Introduce information the viewer feels compelled to discover.

Examples:

- "Nobody told me this was why my ads weren't converting."
- "This tiny detail made the entire video look better."
- "I almost returned this until I figured this out."

The gap between what the audience knows and what they want to know creates continued attention.

---

## 8. Pattern Interrupt Hook

Begin with something visually, verbally, or conceptually unexpected.

Examples:

- An unusual camera angle
- A surprising action
- An unexpected statement
- Showing the final result before explaining it
- Demonstrating the problem dramatically

Example:

"Stop scrolling. Your hook probably isn't the problem."

Pattern interrupts should still be relevant to the ad.

Random shock value may attract attention but reduce buyer quality.

---

## 9. Contrarian Hook

Challenge a commonly held assumption.

Examples:

- "You probably don't need more followers."
- "Stop trying to make your ads look professional."
- "Your product may not be the reason your ads aren't converting."
- "More features can actually make your offer harder to sell."

The next part of the ad must explain why the contrarian statement is reasonable.

Avoid being controversial purely for attention.

---

## 10. Mistake Hook

Tell the viewer they may be doing something incorrectly.

Examples:

- "You're probably using this wrong."
- "Three mistakes ruining your Facebook ads."
- "If you're doing this before applying your makeup, stop."
- "This is the mistake I made when I first started running ads."

Mistake hooks create curiosity and self-evaluation.

They work especially well for educational content.

---

## 11. Warning Hook

Warn the customer about a risk, mistake, or overlooked issue.

Examples:

- "Don't buy a posture corrector before checking this."
- "Before you spend another dollar on ads, look at this."
- "If your landing page does this, you're probably losing customers."

Warnings should be legitimate.

Avoid exaggerated fear or deceptive danger claims.

---

## 12. Question Hook

Ask a question that causes the viewer to mentally answer.

Examples:

- "Why are people clicking your ad but not buying?"
- "What if the problem isn't your product?"
- "Does your makeup look great in the morning and terrible by noon?"
- "How much time does your team spend manually following up with leads?"

Good questions should connect directly to a real customer problem or desire.

Avoid generic questions such as:

"Do you want to improve your life?"

---

## 13. Audience Callout Hook

Directly identify the person the ad is meant for.

Examples:

- "Small business owners running their own Facebook ads..."
- "If you're a woman over 40 trying to build muscle..."
- "For anyone sitting at a desk eight hours a day..."
- "E-commerce brands spending money on Meta ads..."

This increases relevance.

The viewer should immediately think:

"This is for me."

---

## 14. Social Proof Hook

Lead with evidence that other people already use, like, or benefit from the product.

Examples:

- "This product keeps selling out for a reason."
- "After hundreds of customers asked about this, here's how it works."
- "This is the product people kept recommending to me."

Use real evidence.

Never invent:

- Customer counts
- Reviews
- Revenue
- Sales
- Testimonials
- Results

---

## 15. Demonstration Hook

Show the product solving the problem immediately.

Examples:

- Show a stain disappearing
- Show before and after organization
- Demonstrate a tool completing a task
- Show the product being used in an unexpected way

This is especially powerful when the value of the product can be visually understood within seconds.

The demonstration itself can be the hook without needing much dialogue.

---

## 16. Before-and-After Hook

Show the starting state and desired state.

Example:

"Before I changed this..."

Then show:

"After."

Best for products or services with visually obvious transformations.

Avoid misleading before-and-after claims.

---

## 17. Story Hook

Start in the middle of an interesting situation.

Examples:

- "I knew something was wrong when my ads suddenly stopped converting."
- "I almost threw this away."
- "Three months ago, I couldn't figure out why customers kept leaving."
- "I bought this because I thought it was a gimmick."

Stories create unanswered questions:

What happened?
Why?
What happened next?

---

## 18. Confession Hook

Present a personal admission.

Examples:

- "I wasted a lot of money learning this."
- "I didn't want to admit this was actually working."
- "I used to make this mistake constantly."
- "I thought this product was completely unnecessary."

This can feel natural in creator-style or UGC advertising.

It should sound believable rather than scripted.

---

## 19. Comparison Hook

Compare two approaches, products, or outcomes.

Examples:

- "$20 solution vs. $200 solution."
- "Doing this manually vs. automating it."
- "The way I used to edit videos vs. how I do it now."

Comparisons simplify decisions and make differences easier to understand.

---

## 20. "What Nobody Tells You" Hook

Suggest that the viewer is missing important information.

Examples:

- "What nobody tells you about running your first Facebook ad."
- "The part of building muscle nobody explained to me."
- "What most product pages forget."

This combines curiosity with exclusivity.

Do not imply secret knowledge unless the content actually provides something useful.

---

## 21. Negative-Specificity Hook

What it is:

Describe the audience's current problems with high specificity and accuracy rather than making an exaggerated promise about the future.

Be highly specific about the negative situation and more restrained about the positive claim.

Why it works:

Accurately describing someone's problem creates recognition:

"That's exactly what I'm dealing with."

If the creator demonstrates unusually strong understanding of the problem, the audience becomes more likely to believe that the creator may also understand the solution.

This can be more credible than promising dramatic outcomes.

When to use it:

When targeting a clearly defined niche.

When the audience is skeptical of big promises.

When marketing to experienced buyers.

When you deeply understand recurring customer pain points.

Examples:

"You're getting leads, but half never respond and the other half aren't qualified."

"Your videos get views, but almost nobody remembers who made them."

"Your landing page isn't broken — people just don't understand the offer fast enough."

"Your ads are getting clicks while your checkout quietly destroys the conversion."

Common mistakes:

Describing generic problems that apply to everyone.

Using pain points that do not match the target audience.

Overdramatizing the negative situation.

Immediately following the diagnosis with an unrealistic promise.

--- 

## 22. Increasing-Deprivation Hook

What it is

Show the audience that an existing problem becomes worse, more expensive, or harder to fix when ignored.

Core pattern:

You already have [problem], and waiting makes [consequence] worse.

Why it works:

A normal problem creates interest.

A problem that compounds over time creates urgency.

The audience realizes that doing nothing is itself a decision with a cost.

This raises the perceived importance of learning the solution immediately.

When to use it:

When the problem genuinely compounds.

When delays create lost revenue, customers, opportunities, time, or performance.

When discussing operational bottlenecks.

When addressing preventable problems.

Examples:

"Every extra second your website takes to load is costing you customers."

"Ignoring these tracking errors makes your ad data less useful every week."

"The longer you keep generating leads this way, the more money you're wasting."

"This small conversion problem gets much more expensive as your traffic grows."

Common mistakes:

Creating fake urgency.

Claiming consequences grow when they actually remain constant.

Using excessive fear.

Failing to quantify or explain the consequence.

# Advanced Hook Principles

## Avatar-Language Hook Rule

### What it is:

Use terminology that reflects the result and wording the target audience naturally cares about rather than internal or technical terminology.

When uncertain, ask the target audience which phrasing they prefer.

### Why it works:

Different phrases describing the same underlying offer can attract very different people.

Audience language improves recognition and makes the hook feel personally relevant.

It can also improve lead quality because wording acts as a form of audience selection.

### When to use it:

Naming offers or lead magnets.
Writing ads.
Creating social hooks.
Targeting a narrow demographic.
When several phrases could describe the same outcome.

### Examples:

A technical description:

"Six-Week Resistance Training Program"

could become:

"Six Weeks to Stronger, More Defined Glutes"

Likewise:

"Automated CRM Lead-Nurture Workflow"

could become:

"Never Let a Hot Lead Go Cold Again."

### Common mistakes:

Choosing terminology based on what the creator likes.

Using technical language customers rarely use.

Polling people outside the intended audience.

Optimizing purely for the most attention rather than attracting the right customer.


# Short-Form Video Hook Structure

For TikTok, Reels, Shorts, and UGC-style ads, the opening seconds are extremely important.

A useful structure is:

HOOK
↓
PROBLEM OR DESIRE
↓
CURIOSITY / PROOF
↓
SOLUTION
↓
PRODUCT
↓
BENEFITS / DEMONSTRATION
↓
OBJECTION HANDLING
↓
CALL TO ACTION

The hook does not need to explain everything.

Its job is to earn the next few seconds of attention.

---

# Visual Hooks

The first visual matters as much as the first sentence.

Potential visual hooks:

- Product already in use
- Close-up demonstration
- Unexpected movement
- Before-and-after
- Strong facial expression
- Unboxing
- Showing the problem
- Showing the end result first
- Large change in environment
- Someone interacting directly with the product
- Screenshot or visual proof
- Unusual framing

Avoid opening with:

- Long logos
- Slow introductions
- Empty establishing shots
- Someone saying "Hey guys"
- Generic product beauty shots with no context

Unless the brand itself already has strong recognition.

---

# Hook + Visual Congruence

The spoken hook and visual should reinforce each other.

Example:

Spoken:
"If your makeup looks like this after four hours..."

Visual:
Close-up showing makeup separating.

This is stronger than:

Spoken:
"If your makeup looks like this after four hours..."

Visual:
Person sitting at a desk doing nothing.

The viewer should immediately understand why the visual is relevant.

---

# Hook Specificity Formula

A useful hook formula is:

[AUDIENCE] + [SPECIFIC PROBLEM/DESIRE] + [CURIOSITY OR PROMISE]

Example:

"If you're running Facebook ads but getting clicks without purchases, check this before changing your targeting."

Audience:
Facebook advertisers

Problem:
Clicks but no purchases

Curiosity:
There may be another cause

Promise:
The ad will explain what to check

---

# Identity-Based Hooks

People pay more attention to messages that feel personally relevant.

Examples:

- "For people who hate going to the gym..."
- "If you're the person who always forgets to..."
- "Busy founders who don't have time to manually follow up..."
- "For women trying to build muscle without living in the gym..."

Identity hooks should describe the audience accurately rather than insult them.

---

# Emotional Hooks

Strong advertising often connects to emotion.

Possible emotional drivers include:

- Frustration
- Embarrassment
- Confidence
- Relief
- Convenience
- Fear of wasting money
- Desire for control
- Status
- Belonging
- Curiosity
- Excitement
- Hope

The emotion should naturally connect to the customer's problem or goal.

Avoid manipulating people using extreme fear, shame, or false urgency.

---

# Benefit vs. Feature Hooks

Features explain what a product has.

Benefits explain why the customer should care.

Feature:
"Made with adjustable straps."

Benefit:
"Adjust it until it feels comfortable for your body."

Feature:
"AI-powered lead follow-up."

Benefit:
"Every new lead gets a response even when your team is busy."

Hooks usually work better when they lead with the benefit instead of the technical feature.

---

# Strong vs. Weak Hook Examples

Weak:
"Check out our amazing new product."

Strong:
"If your desk job leaves your shoulders feeling like this every night, watch this."

Weak:
"Our software uses artificial intelligence."

Strong:
"This automatically follows up with leads your sales team forgot about."

Weak:
"We sell premium skincare."

Strong:
"If your skin feels dry again 20 minutes after moisturizing, this may be why."

Weak:
"We offer marketing automation."

Strong:
"Your team shouldn't be manually sending the same follow-up email 40 times a day."

# Hook Testing

Never assume one hook will always win.

For the same advertisement, test multiple hooks while keeping the rest of the ad similar.

Example:

Ad concept:
Posture support product

Hook A:
"If your back hurts after sitting all day, watch this."

Hook B:
"I thought my chair was causing my back pain."

Hook C:
"Desk workers: you may be doing this without realizing it."

Hook D:
"I tested this for a week because I thought it was a gimmick."

Hook E:
"Before you buy another expensive office chair, try this."

Testing different hooks helps reveal which angle creates the strongest response.

---

# Hook Variation Strategy

When generating multiple hooks, do not simply rewrite the same sentence.

Test genuinely different psychological angles.

Possible variations:

1. Problem
2. Desired outcome
3. Curiosity
4. Contrarian belief
5. Personal story
6. Demonstration
7. Mistake
8. Warning
9. Social proof
10. Audience callout

Example product:
Portable blender

Problem:
"Tired of cleaning an entire blender just to make one shake?"

Desired outcome:
"Make a protein shake anywhere in less than a minute."

Curiosity:
"I stopped using my normal blender after trying this."

Demonstration:
"Watch this blend frozen fruit in seconds."

Identity:
"Gym people who hate washing blenders, this is for you."

---

# Hook Evaluation Checklist

Before using a hook, evaluate it.

Ask:

1. Is the target customer immediately clear?
2. Does it address a real problem or desire?
3. Is it specific?
4. Does it create curiosity?
5. Is the claim believable?
6. Does the first visual support the hook?
7. Does the rest of the ad deliver on the promise?
8. Is it different from generic advertising language?
9. Can the viewer understand it immediately?
10. Does it attract potential customers rather than merely viewers?

# Common Hook Mistakes

## Being Too Generic

Weak:
"You need this."

Better:
"If you spend half your workday manually replying to leads, this could save you hours."

---

## Taking Too Long to Get to the Point

Avoid:

"Hey guys, today I wanted to come on here because I've been getting a lot of questions..."

Instead:

"I tested this for seven days. Here's what happened."

---

## Starting With the Brand

Unless the brand itself is famous, the viewer may not care yet.

Weak:
"At XYZ Company, we believe..."

Better:
"If your sales team is losing leads because they respond too slowly..."

Introduce the brand after earning attention.

---

## Using Fake Curiosity

Do not create mystery around something trivial.

Example:

"You'll NEVER believe what happened..."

If the answer is ordinary, trust decreases.

---

## Making Unrealistic Claims

Avoid hooks such as:

"Lose 20 pounds in 7 days."

"Make $10,000 overnight."

"This guarantees your ads will go viral."

Hooks should be persuasive without being deceptive.

---

## Optimizing Only for Views

A hook can attract massive attention while attracting the wrong audience.

Example:

A shocking celebrity reference might generate views but bring people who have no interest in the product.

The goal is not simply:

MORE ATTENTION

The goal is:

RELEVANT ATTENTION
+
CONTINUED INTEREST
+
BUYING INTENT

---

# Hook Creation Framework

When creating hooks for an advertisement:

Step 1:
Identify the target customer.

Step 2:
Identify their strongest problem or desired outcome.

Step 3:
Identify the most interesting product benefit.

Step 4:
Choose several hook categories.

Step 5:
Write multiple hooks for each category.

Step 6:
Remove generic wording.

Step 7:
Make each hook more specific.

Step 8:
Check that the hook naturally connects to the rest of the advertisement.

Step 9:
Choose multiple hooks to test rather than relying on one.

# Agent Instructions for Using This File

When generating hooks:

- Generate genuinely different concepts, not minor wording changes.
- Match the hook to the platform and customer awareness level.
- Prefer customer-centered language over brand-centered language.
- Avoid exaggerated or unsupported claims.
- Consider both the spoken hook and opening visual.
- Make hooks easy to understand immediately.
- Use specific language when possible.
- Prioritize relevant attention over empty views.
- Generate multiple psychological angles when testing.
- Ensure the rest of the advertisement fulfills the hook's promise.

When analyzing hooks:

Evaluate:

- Attention strength
- Relevance
- Specificity
- Curiosity
- Emotional connection
- Credibility
- Visual compatibility
- Product relevance
- Audience fit
- Conversion potential
